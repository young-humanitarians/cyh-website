      body:
        - "And something has changed since the last peak of this curve. War has always had a
          physical dimension and a psychological one. It now has a third one, a digital
          dimension fought through information, norms, and narratives across screens and
          communities. In this dimension, the rules quietly get rewritten. Shared humanitarian
          norms erode. Violations that once provoked outrage are met with fatigue. International
          Humanitarian Law (IHL) is applied selectively. The baseline shifts: what was once
          unthinkable, slowly becomes normal."
        - That third dimension is the reason this concerns all of us, including those of us who
          live in safe and privileged countries like Switzerland. It reaches us daily when we
          open our phones. A hospital bombed, sometimes seconds after it happened. The most
          vulnerable people named as targets. World leaders speaking about entire populations in
          a way we would not accept at our own dinner table. And underneath all of it, a
          question we can no longer answer. Is what I am seeing even true, or is it a deepfake,
          or somebody's political agenda?
  - discriminant: text
    value:

      heading: How does this reach all of us?
      body:
        - On a personal level, we disengage. We put the phone down, not because we stopped
          caring, but because we do not know what to do with the caring.
        - 'And that does something to us. Empathy shrinks. People who are not like us slowly
          become "the others". Research backs this up: dehumanising language enters our feeds,
          then our conversations, and the moral baseline quietly moves.'
        - On a societal level, trust collapses. Trust is the raw material of every form of
          cooperation between countries and institutions. When it goes, we get less support,
          less funding and less accountability for the people who break the rules.

      body:
        - Traditionally, ways to contribute — activism, volunteering, civic engagement, or
          financial support — have largely been one-directional. Valuable as they are, they
          don't work for everyone. Some people can't afford to give money. Others feel that
          protesting can deepen the polarisation they want to end. Many simply don't have the
          time to volunteer. As a result, we lose a large group of people who want to contribute
          but don't have a way that works for them.
        - The CYH opens a second direction, and it has two parts.
